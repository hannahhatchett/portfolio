Situation

Optimizely customers say they want personalization. Almost none of them ship it. The people who buy the product are not the people who have to run it, and the handoff between them breaks.

The buyer signs the contract and moves on. Launching a campaign falls to a marketer who never chose the goal. The marketers hit a data setup wall the sales conversation never mentioned. Even after clearing it, they have no reliable way to tell whether the campaign worked without product knowledge. Personalization offers nothing like the statistical confidence marketers already trust from A/B testing.

Most customers who buy Optimizely's personalization product are not using it, and the churn rate after the first campaign is around 80-90%.

Optimizely SME

That gap between intent and usage became the project. It is not what we were originally asked to work on.

Task

Our advisors brought us in to build a personalization agent for Opal, Optimizely's agentic platform. The goal was to help clients carry out more tasks inside a product they already owned. It named a destination and left discovery to fill in the details.

Action

What we designed is an activation workflow. The personalization product already existed. The work was getting new customers into it, and giving the customers already in it a reason to stay.

Those are two audiences and one surface. A marketer who has never launched a campaign needs a way in that does not start with a manual data audit. A marketer already running campaigns needs a prompt they would not have gone looking for — a nudge that says "here is something you can run right now," or "here is something you could run after connecting two more data points." The first group gets activated, the second gets a quality of life update, and Optimizely gets a product its customers actually use.

The project moved through three phases. Our discovery phase ended by changing the brief. Two design phases followed, and I owned a surface in each one. Research reframed what we were solving, and the design work had to answer the two findings with the least product support behind them.

We treated personalization as a mode inside the experimentation workflow marketers already use, not as its own destination. That answered a finding we kept hearing, where personalization and experimentation are seen as two parallels in the same workflow. This led to a constraint for my own design work. Whatever I designed had to fit inside a workflow that marketers are already familiar with.

Phase 01

Discovery & Reframing the Brief

I needed to understand how marketers actually do personalization, and why interest so rarely turns into a shipped campaign. I also needed enough evidence to confirm the original direction or argue for a different one.

As a team, we ran 11 interviews. Six were Optimizely subject matter experts and five were marketers, some of them Optimizely customers and some on competitor tools. Alongside that we did desk research on Optimizely's existing personalization and experimentation products. I ran 4 of the 11 interviews, then pulled insights and findings.

Our original brief was to explore agents for task automation, based on our advisors' initial assumption. What we found didn't support that: the problem wasn't task automation at all. It was churn. Customers found the personalization product hard to use and navigate. They could not work out what data it needed from them. Three findings anchored everything that came after.

Customers think personalization requires a complete customer profile. Marketers assumed they needed a full CDP (Customer Data Platform) setup before they could start, so they never discovered the campaigns they could already run with the data they had.

Personalization struggles when it's treated as separate from experimentation. A different tool and a different mental model meant it competed for attention behind testing work marketers were already prioritizing.

Testing was simpler than personalization… personalization felt like the next step I was trying to get to.

Marketer

Customers can't judge whether personalization worked. Without a clear read on success, marketers could not tell if a campaign changed anything, especially when the value showed up in behavior rather than an immediate lift in conversions.

We took this back to our advisors and pushed to change the scope. We proposed a fix inside the personalization product itself, aiming at the churn our research found, rather than an agent layered on top of it. Building the original agent would have added capability to a product customers were already abandoning.

The advisors agreed and the project refocused. The three findings turned into three design opportunities. The first was to give customers an entry point using data they already have. The second was to make personalization part of the experimentation workflow instead of a separate destination. The third was to show behavioral data next to hard numbers so that success becomes readable.

Phase 02

The Data Gap Picker

Finding one says marketers stall because they think they need a complete customer profile before they can start. The obvious response is to show the full campaign gallery. That actually makes it worse. A marketer scanning everything they could theoretically run still can't tell what is available to them right now, and that uncertainty is what stopped them in the first place.

I needed to design an entry point that gives a marketer one campaign they can launch today, without requiring them to understand their entire data setup first.

The picker leads with campaigns they can run today on data already connected. Below that it shows near miss campaigns that sit one or two data points away, each with a visible path to unlock them. A marketer never has to know what a CDP is, or whether their setup counts as complete. They see what works now and what sits one step away.

The same surface keeps working after the first campaign. As a marketer connects more data, near miss campaigns cross over into runnable ones, and the nudge surfaces them without being asked. Someone six campaigns in gets told what just became possible.

Phase 03

The Results Pages

Finding three had the least product support behind it. A/B testing has trained marketers to expect a result to come with evidence behind it, and to distrust one that arrives without any. Personalization offered them nothing equivalent. A flat conversion number does not answer the question they are actually asking, which is whether they should believe it.

I needed to design how a result is presented, which meant deciding what a marketer sees first, in what order, and with how much confidence attached to it. The page had to give an honest read on whether a campaign moved their metric, even when the honest answer was that there was not enough data yet.

My work here was presentation: deciding what a marketer sees first, what gets qualified, and what the page refuses to claim. How a result reads changes with sample size. A campaign at roughly 100 visitors gets flagged as too early to trust. One nearing 1,000 gets a steady read on the trend. States like "no holdback configured" or a tracking fault get called out plainly instead of showing up as a zero. A zero a marketer can't tell apart from a real result is worse than no number at all.

Where it helps, the page shows behavioral signal next to lift, so a marketer can see engagement shift before conversions move — a conversion number alone can't answer that part of finding three.

As of today, these pages are still in refinement. The presentation logic is settled; I'm still adding elements as my user testing feedback comes in.

Result

This project is still in development. I am currently in the final round of user testing and refinement, before the project closes at the end of August. Outside of the prototype, the remaining work is a video demo of the full flow, shot like a commercial, which I am directing.

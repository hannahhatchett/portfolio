Situation

Haunted Heist is a PvP horror party game from Autotroph Games, a two person studio in Seattle that brings on contractors for specific pieces of work throughout development. Heisters race to collect gems in a mansion while Tricksters use chaotic abilities to derail them and raise their fright score for a win.

The parts of a game that get playtested most are the ones players notice: combat, movement, multiplayer, the core loop. Settings, the lobby, and the wait between matches get touched by every player but rarely get the same attention. They're infrastructure, not features, so they're easy to overlook until a player is stuck in one.

That matters most at launch. A wishlist isn't a player yet. It's someone who will decide in their first few minutes whether to keep going or to refund the game. Menus that keep a player stuck lead to refunds.

Task

I joined the project in May as a UX consultant, working directly under Autotroph's designer. I owned the settings menu redesign end to end, from information architecture to readability. I also contributed to the design direction for the lobby and the loading screen experience.

Action

A player should never have to relearn a game's logic. In settings, that means every tab behaves the same way as the last one. In the lobby, it means you act on the thing you want directly instead of navigating toward it. In loading, it means the wait tells you something about the role you're about to play.

Phase 01

Settings Menu Redesign

The settings menu had grown a tab at a time, and each tab had been built to its own logic. Video had no grouping between display settings and quality settings, its header text shrank inconsistently below Graphics, and it had no reset. Audio had no section headers at all, so voice chat controls sat mixed in with master volume. Gameplay was the only tab with section headers, which was correct, and which also made it the odd one out. Controls put sensitivity and keybinds in one list.

The result was a menu that asked players to relearn its logic every time they switched tabs. I needed one structure that held across all four tabs, organized around what a player is actually looking for rather than how the settings happened to be filed.

I started by screenshotting every tab and annotating it directly, marking where the hierarchy broke down and where a tab did something the others didn't. Once that audit was done, I studied the settings menus of 5 other multiplayer games to see how their hierarchies were structured and how they presented them.

Between the audit and that pass, I landed on three principles. Same form on every tab, meaning one header pattern, one footer, one grouping logic, and one density regardless of where a player lands. Group by player intent rather than system category. And speak the game's language, using the chunky outlines, category color, and typography the rest of the game already uses for brand affirming, visual consistency.

The new information architecture subgroups every tab.

I kept this pass on information architecture and interaction rather than final visual polish. Restructuring how the menu is organized before deciding how it should look is the order that keeps a visual pass from having to redo structural work later. Autotroph approved this direction outright.

Phase 02

The Loading Screen

In a multiplayer match every player loads at a different speed. Someone is always waiting on a teammate with slower wifi, looking at a blank screen with nothing to read and no sense of what's coming. I needed the wait to do something for the player instead of just passing.

I proposed a loading screen with room for the player's username and role title, splash art that sets tone and carries the branding, and tips written for the role you were assigned. A reminder to conserve ammo, for instance, because you never know when you'll need to defend yourself from Tricksters. The screen orients you to your role before the match starts, so the time spent waiting is time spent getting ready.

The direction is proposed and the piece is in active iteration and development.

Phase 03

The Lobby Menu

The lobby had two problems that made each other worse. Moving between roles relies on arrows instead of letting players pick a role directly, which is slower and less obvious than it needs to be. And there was no confirmation before leaving, so a player could exit the entire match without warning. The auto assign button sits close enough to the navigation arrows that it could easily cause a misclick.

My first direction in ideation had player models lined up, with room for players to emote and have fun while their friends readied up. I decided against it, though, once I brought it back to a constraint from Autotroph: the lobby needed to account for spectators, and spectator count isn't capped, so the layout would've had to work with an unpredictable, potentially unlimited number of models on screen. That constraint pushed me toward direct manipulation.

The arrows are gone, and every role can be selected directly. Hovering another player's character lets you adjust their mic volume from that spot, rather than through settings. This was mine end to end: desk research on multiplayer game frustrations kept surfacing the same complaint, that players had no way to raise or lower a specific friend's volume. I built it into the lobby specifically because that's the first place a player would actually notice someone's volume was off, right as the group is forming. Putting it there also kept the fix inside a player's existing flow of starting a match, instead of asking them to break away into settings to solve it.

The crown marking the host moved from beside the role UI to above their head, so it matches how other status indicators already work. Auto Assign and Ready Up moved from the middle of the role selection flow, so a misclick no longer interrupts.

One decision here is worth calling out. I removed the Leave button from this screen. My reasoning going in was that Leave was ambiguous in this context, since it could read as leaving the menu or leaving the match, and a player who reads it the wrong way drops out of a game they meant to stay in.

I ran a moderated test with 3 users because I wanted the decision backed by data rather than my own read of the ambiguity. Each player accidentally left the lobby when they meant to leave the menu.

Autotroph pushed back on part of this direction. They liked most of the proposal, but they want to keep the original concept's head to head symmetry between Heisters and Tricksters, which my layout moved away from. We're now working on a version that combines both, their symmetry with my interaction model.

Result

Haunted Heist releases October 12. Some of this work is in the build and some is still in development.
